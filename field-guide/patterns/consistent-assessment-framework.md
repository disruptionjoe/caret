# Consistent Assessment Framework

## Notation

```
^ CCF
  ^ Containment
  ^ Coherence
  ^ Flow

  [applied to every item]
  [applied to every domain]
```

Three dimensions. Used everywhere. Simplicity creates comparability.

## What it does

Defines a small set of assessment dimensions (three is ideal) and applies them consistently to every item and every domain. CCF: Containment, Coherence, Flow. Every task in priority.md gets scored on these three. Every domain in coach.md gets scored on the same three. The dimensions are stable. The domains change. The framework stays.

This creates a common language. You can compare a task to a domain. You can compare two tasks. You can compare two domains. All on the same axis.

## When to use it

When you need to evaluate heterogeneous things on a common scale. Tasks, domains, projects, proposals. When you want consistency without endless rubrics.

When you want to spot patterns. If all your domains are failing on Coherence, that's information. If only one task is failing on Containment, that's different information.

When you want the assessment to be fast. Three dimensions: fast. Twelve dimensions: slow.

## When not to use it

When the things you're evaluating are fundamentally different and require different criteria. Don't force heterogeneous things into the same framework if the framework adds noise.

When simplicity would hide critical nuance. If you need ten dimensions to capture real differences, use ten. But push hard against adding the eleventh.

## Design notes

Consistent assessment framework is a forcing function for simplicity. It says: you get three dimensions. Choose them carefully. Everything must fit.

The discipline is in the choice. Containment, Coherence, Flow works because each dimension captures a real failure mode.

**Containment:** Is the scope clear. Do you know where this starts and stops. A task with poor containment spills into adjacent work. A domain with poor containment has blurry boundaries with neighboring domains. High containment means you could implement or isolate this thing without touching ten other things.

**Coherence:** Does this hang together as a single thing or is it a pile. Coherence is internal alignment. A task with high coherence has steps that flow from one to the next. A domain with high coherence has a unifying theme, not a random collection of work.

**Flow:** Can work progress or is it stuck. Flow is about velocity and dependencies. A task with high flow has clear next steps. A domain with high flow is not bottlenecked on external decision or resources. Flow is not about doing it fast. It's about not being stuck.

These three dimensions work on everything because they're about structure, not content. You could apply them to a software project, a writing project, a legal case, a sales pipeline. The structure questions are the same.

Scoring is usually five levels. High (5), good (4), okay (3), weak (2), broken (1). Not granular. Not deceptive precision. Five levels. You can argue about 3 vs 4. You cannot argue about 2 vs 4.

Implementation: every item in priority.md has CCF scores. Every domain in coach.md has CCF scores. Every quarter goal has CCF scores. The scores are updated on a regular cadence. Weekly for active items. Monthly for domains. Quarterly for long-term goals.

The scoring is visible and defensible. You write a sentence for each dimension explaining the score. "Containment is 4 because the scope is clear but we haven't fully defined acceptance criteria." Not just a number. A number plus explanation.

One pattern that emerges: items that are 5-5-3 (high, high, low flow) often need help. They're well-structured but stuck. Identify the blocker. Items that are 3-2-4 (okay containment, weak coherence, high flow) are suspicious. They're moving but in the wrong direction. Clarify the direction.

Comparing domains is powerful. If Engineering is 4-4-4 and Sales is 2-3-2, you know where to focus. Sales has structural problems. Engineering is humming. This comparison would be impossible with different frameworks for each domain.

Tracking changes over time shows patterns. A domain that slowly drifts from 4-4-4 to 3-3-3 is declining. Not catastrophic. But declining. You can catch it and intervene. Without the framework, the decline is invisible until it's a crisis.

The framework also prevents arguments about priorities. Two domains both want resources. One is 5-5-4, one is 2-2-2. The framework gives you the conversation. "Here's why I'm focusing on Sales: it's failing on fundamental structure. We need to fix that before we add more velocity."

A critical detail: the framework is not aspirational. You're not scoring what it should be. You're scoring what it is right now. A domain that needs a full rewrite is a 2. You don't pretend it's a 4 once you're done. You track the work to improve it. The scores move as the reality changes.

Another detail: the framework is stable. Resist the urge to add a fourth dimension. You'll add a fifth. Then a sixth. Soon you have a rubric that's useless because nothing is comparable. Defend three. Three is hard.

Calibration is important. Every three months, review the framework. Are we scoring consistently. Does a 4 mean the same thing in March and June. Drift is normal. Recalibration is the fix.

The pattern scales because it's binary in application: everything gets assessed on the same framework. No exceptions. No "this domain is too complex for CCF." Complexity actually makes the framework more useful, not less. Complexity hides structure. CCF exposes it.

One more note: the framework works because it's in service of decision-making, not explanation. The point is not to understand why something is a 2. The point is to decide what to do about it. If the framework helps you make better decisions faster, it's working.
